01 / The starting point

Invisible ideas.
Tangible experiences.

Quantum ideas are hard to see. Play makes them tangible.

My work as a content contributor and mind-map designer at Q-JAM involved organizing quantum knowledge, collecting analogies, and mapping concepts for learning activities. That work led to the question behind Quantum Adventure:

How might play help students build intuition before they encounter the equations?

Q-JAM preparation and personal reflection · Original project, p. 2. The camp ran May–August 2025; the portfolio lists the wider project through September.

02 / Research → direction

Curiosity first.
Complexity later.

Three research signals shaped the direction. Open a card to trace the decision.

01 / Accessibility“Will I understand it?”+

Student interviews showed curiosity alongside difficulty with physics jargon.

→ Start with familiar actions, then connect them to the scientific terms.
02 / LearningUnderstand by doing.+

Constructivism, experiential learning, and situated learning informed a cycle of doing, reflecting, and adjusting.

→ Let learners make choices and see consequences together.
03 / EngagementGive the idea a role.+

Interview summaries described interest in board games, puzzles, and logic.

→ Build quantum ideas into the mechanics, rather than add them as trivia.

Research and four student interview summaries · Original project, p. 3. Card headlines are editorial summaries, not participant quotes.

04 / An interactive rules study

Place a possibility.
See what happens.

Choose a piece, then click a space to place it. Roll to move, or try a concept directly. Two people can explore together on one device.

About this web adaptation

This is a learning sandbox based on the original project’s documented board effects (p. 8). The 40-space layout is rearranged for the web. Free placement, manual measurement-outcome selection, and the scenario buttons are exploration controls added for this portfolio. Entanglement counts down over three shared demo rolls. Energy stations award one energy per active token, as described in the source.

The full card deck, card dealing, special corner rules, and a competitive win condition are not reproduced. The original rules say “first to 10 points”; this sandbox tracks energy without assuming that points and energy are interchangeable. Events use only the documented +1-energy example. This demo is not evidence of user testing or learning effectiveness.

05 / Testing & iteration

Less explanation.
More understanding.

Prototype playtesting exposed friction in reading cards, tracking resources, and understanding the tokens. The design changed in response.

Click to see what changed

01Word-heavy cards+
Before

Players could not read card effects quickly.

After

Icons + keywords

Clear visual cues replaced long instructions.

02Progress was easy to lose+
Before

Players forgot collected energy and had no way to track loops.

After

Physical trackers

A personal energy tracker and a round counter made state visible.

03Identical tokens+
Before

Superposition was difficult to visualize.

After

A dual-piece design

Custom tokens visually expressed two simultaneous states.

Making the state visible.

Final-playtest feedback described the two-token mechanic as distinctive and entanglement as a source of shared benefit and risk.

These reactions informed the design narrative. The available portfolio does not provide enough methodological detail to claim a validated improvement in learning.

Prototype issues, iterations, and final-playtest reactions · pp. 5–7.

06 / Reflection

A metaphor is a doorway.
Not the whole theory.

The strongest learning interactions make an idea visible without pretending to explain everything.

This project connects my interests in psychology, learning, and interaction design: how people build intuition, how feedback changes understanding, and how a physical system can support conversation.

Where I’d take it next

Test whether learners can explain the concepts after play, check where the metaphors create misconceptions with a physics educator, and refine the rules with learners who have different science backgrounds.

The next steps above are proposed follow-up work.
